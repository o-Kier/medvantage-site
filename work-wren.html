Wren: a dental website that answers “what will it cost?” before anyone has to ask.

Most dental websites hide their fees and lead with stock smiles. Wren puts prices, people and plain English first, and keeps the dental advertising rules in view throughout.

1

The brief

Patients choose a dentist on two questions: will I be looked after, and what will it cost? The site has to answer both in the first scroll.

We set out to design a site for an independent private practice in Clapham, competing with corporate chains for the same patients. It needed to feel bright, modern and kind, turn nervous visitors into booked check-ups, and be written with GDC guidance and UK advertising rules in mind from the first draft.

2

What dental sites usually get wrong

Most practices are better than their websites suggest. The same gaps come up again and again.

  • Fees hidden away

    “Price on consultation” everywhere, so patients leave to find a practice that tells them.

  • Faces without names

    Stock smiles and team pages with no names, roles or registration details.

  • Titles that overreach

    “Specialist” and “Dr” used loosely, when both carry specific meanings for dentists.

  • Booking built for the practice

    Phone-only, office hours only, and nothing for the patient who is nervous about calling.

3

Key decisions

Each section answers one question a patient has before they pick up the phone.

01

Reassurance before anything else

The headline promises calm and clarity, not perfection. Two small cards answer the practical questions straight away: when is it open, and what does a first visit cost? Real facts replace the usual made-up star ratings and “10,000 happy smiles”.

02

Treatments in soft, simple tiles

Six treatments, each with a one-line explanation and a “from” price. Cosmetic options are described honestly: aligners are confirmed at consultation, and implants take place over several months.

03

Fees on the page

A full fee guide sits on the homepage, beside a simple monthly membership plan. It’s the question every patient has and few practices answer, so answering it becomes the reason to book.

04

Names you’ll know, registers you can check

Every clinician is named with their role and qualifications, and the page explains that “Dr” is a courtesy title for dentists, with a link to search the GDC register.

05

A first visit designed for nervous patients

Plenty of adults put off the dentist because they’re anxious, so nervousness gets its own path: a link in the fact strip, a section on what the first visit is like, and a tick box on the booking form to ask for extra time.

06

Fresh, not clinical

Sage and white feel clean without the cold blue of most dental sites, with soft sky and sand tints to tell the treatments apart. A single friendly sans-serif keeps it modern and easy to read. Every text colour meets WCAG AA contrast.

  • White#FFFFFF
  • Sage#DBE7C5
  • Sky#E6EEF6
  • Ink#151A12
4

Compliance, designed in

Dental marketing is shaped by GDC guidance, the CAP Code and CQC registration. Rather than checking a finished site against them, we wrote the site around them.

“Specialist”
Kept for dentists on the GDC’s specialist lists. Wren’s implant dentist has “a particular interest” instead.
“Dr”
Explained on the page as a courtesy title, so no one mistakes a dentist for a medical doctor.
Registration
Every clinician named, with a direct link to search the GDC register.
CQC
The CQC inspects dental practices but doesn’t rate them, so the site links to the inspection report rather than showing a rating badge.
Claims about results
No “painless”, “perfect” or “guaranteed”. Aligners and implants come with honest notes on suitability and time.
Reviews and names
Reviews describe the experience, not the outcome, and treatments use plain names rather than trade names.

This is how we approach copy, not legal advice. For a real practice, the final wording is always reviewed with the dentists who stand behind it.

5

Built for the phone first

Most patients look for a dentist on their phone, often when something already hurts. The treatment tiles stack into a single scroll, fees stay readable at a glance, and the call-back form fits on one screen.

6

What a real practice would add

Wren is a concept, so some things are deliberately left out. For a real practice, we’d build these in with the team:

  • Photography of their own surgery, team and patients, with signed consent
  • GDC numbers beside every name, linked to the register
  • A link to their own CQC inspection report
  • Online booking connected to their practice software
  • Reviews drawn from an independent platform
  • Clear notes on NHS and private care, accessibility and complaints
